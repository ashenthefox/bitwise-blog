# Sources

Where each rule comes from, how much of the source was actually read, and what was left out
because it could not be checked. Read this when a rule needs its evidence, or before adding a
claim to the skill.

## How much to trust each kind of source

- **Experiments** say what worked on average under the conditions tested: short lessons, mostly
  novices, mostly immediate tests, often one topic. Most of the studies below use one mechanism
  (a flush cistern, car brakes, soldering, a TV stand). Treat the rules as defaults to test on a
  page, not laws.
- **Statements in an introduction** are the authors' summary of earlier work. They are cited as
  "stated in the introduction", not as that study's finding.
- **Practitioners' advice** is their account of their own method.
- The per-principle effect sizes Mayer reports are likely high: `pedagogical-writing`'s
  [sources](../../pedagogical-writing/references/sources.md) notes an independent meta-analysis
  that pooled g = 0.37 across the multimedia principles.

## Research read in full

Text extracted from the PDF and read. A second-pass row names the sections that were read;
the rest of that text was not.

| Source | What the rules take from it | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt (2002), "Animation: can it facilitate?", *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies* 57, 247–262 | **Congruence Principle**: "the structure and content of the external representation should correspond to the desired structure and content of the internal representation". **Apprehension Principle**: it "should be readily and accurately perceived and comprehended". Where animation beats a static graphic, "the animated graphics convey more information or involve interactivity". Animations "should lean toward the schematic and away from the realistic", with arrows or highlighting to direct attention. "Judicious use of interactivity may overcome both these disadvantages." | A review, not an experiment. It does not say animation never helps: "may be more effective … for real time reorientations in time and space". |
| Larkin & Simon (1987), "Why a Diagram is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words", *Cognitive Science* 11, 65–99 | In a diagram "information is organized by location, and often much of the information needed to make an inference is present and explicit at a single location"; cues to the next step may sit at an adjacent location, so the work is "a smooth traversal of the diagram" with little search. Informationally equivalent representations differ in computational efficiency. | Their models are production systems on physics and mathematics problems (pulleys, geometry), not students. Read from a scanned PDF; its OCR text layer is noisy, so quotes were taken from the abstract. |
| Heiser, Phan, Agrawala, Tversky & Hanrahan (2004), "Identification and Validation of Cognitive Design Principles for Automated Generation of Assembly Instructions", *AVI '04* | The list of principles (see [process-diagrams.md](process-diagrams.md)): one diagram per major step with none omitted; explicit order; parts added visible; mode of attachment not occluded; action diagrams rather than structural; consistent arrows and guidelines; avoid changing viewpoints; stable orientations. Instructions made from them cut assembly time by 35% on average and error by 50% against factory instructions. | **One task**: assembling a TV stand. The paper says "7 principles" and lists eight bullets. The step to "other kinds of visualizations, particularly those that show processes over time" is the authors' belief, not a test. |
| Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller (1999), "Managing Split-attention and Redundancy in Multimedia Instruction", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 13, 351–371 | Integration of text into the diagram "is required if these sources of information are unintelligible in isolation". If the text is "merely redescribing" what a self-contained diagram shows, integrating it adds load and "elimination, rather than integration, of redundant sources of information is beneficial". Experiment 2: colour coding between text and diagram parts reduced the search for referents. | Experiment 1's redundancy was the **same text** in audio and visual form. The diagram-and-text redundancy claim is the authors' summary of earlier studies. 34 first-year trade apprentices, soldering. The expertise-reversal finding is **not in this paper**. |
| Mayer & Gallini (1990), "When is an illustration worth ten thousand words?", *J. Educational Psychology* 82, 715–726. Second pass: the scanned article from a web-archive copy of CiteSeerX; abstract, predictions, method of Experiment 1, results of all three and the conclusion read | Three experiments (brakes, pumps, generators; 96, 96 and 108 students). Only illustrations with parts and steps together, in "a series of two (or more) frames", beat text alone. "These results were obtained mainly for the low prior-knowledge (rather than the high prior-knowledge) students." Low-knowledge readers' problem solving: 43% against 24%, 47% against 28%, 56% against 29%; high-knowledge readers' did not differ in any experiment. Verbatim retention did not improve. The four conditions it tests, which it takes from Mayer's earlier work: a text that explains a cause-and-effect system, tests of understanding, illustrations that explain, and learners who lack experience. | The low-knowledge comparisons rest on groups of 12 to 17. Experiment 3 had no steps-only group. Prior knowledge was self-reported experience. The high-knowledge groups also differed on explanative recall in Experiments 2 and 3. Verbatim retention was tested for low-knowledge readers only. "Dynamic" means frames on paper: there was no animation. It shows the illustrations were not needed by high-knowledge readers, not that they hurt them, and it says nothing about compact figures. The scan's OCR is poor; one page was read by column. |
| Ainsworth (2006), "DeFT: A conceptual framework for considering learning with multiple representations", *Learning and Instruction* 16, 183–198. Second pass: the **author's manuscript** (22 pages and a cover sheet) from a web-archive copy of her upload; sections 2, 5, 6, 7.1, 7.3, 7.5 and 8 read | Three functions of several representations: "to complement, constrain and construct". "It seems wise to use the minimum number of representations consistent with the pedagogical function." "Learners find translating between representations difficult." The relation is easier to see with "consistent labels" and "the same colours to represent the same objects". For the complementary function learners "need not understand the relation between them". | Wording may differ from the journal's, and its page numbers are not known. She calls the advice "heuristics for guiding experimentation not as cast in stone principles". One cited study found that help with translation benefited only readers of middling prior knowledge. "Say what each is for" is the skill's wording, not hers. Her 2008 chapter (in Gilbert et al., *Visualization: Theory and Practice in Science Education*) was read for one point: a constraining representation "is not necessary if the learner has understood the second representation". |
| Hegarty, "Individual Differences in Interacting with Hypermedia Manuals", final report of ONR grant N00014-97-1-0601 (2003), DTIC ADA426950. Second pass: the OCR text of archive.org's copy, read in full | Comprehension "from static and animated diagrams was quite limited, and often resulted in misconceptions about how the systems worked". Three diagrams of phases taught as much as an animation of about 140 frames, and both beat a single diagram. "Learning from static diagrams and text was equivalent to learning from animated diagrams and commentaries, when these materials contained the same information." Verbal description and prediction questions improved comprehension. Attentional cues changed where readers looked and not what they understood. | A grant report that summarises its experiments: no sample sizes or statistics except one study of seven people. It found "no evidence that different formats of instruction were more or less effective for individuals with different abilities and knowledge", which counts against tailoring a figure's format to the reader's knowledge. OCR typos were corrected in the quotes. |
| Mayer & Fiorella (2014), "Principles for reducing extraneous processing in multimedia learning", ch. 12 of *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (2nd ed.), 279–315. Second pass: the typeset chapter from a university course site; abstract, method, the coherence and signalling sections, limitations and both tables read | Coherence: 23 of 23 tests, median d = 0.86. Signalling: 24 of 28, median d = 0.41; visual cues alone d = 0.36, "with stronger effects for certain forms of cueing such as spreading color and weaker effects for other forms such as arrows". Signalling applies more strongly "when it is used sparingly rather than excessively" and "when the display is complex rather than simple". Redundancy 16 of 16, 0.86. Spatial contiguity 22 of 22, 1.10. Temporal contiguity 9 of 9, 1.22. | The measure is Cohen's d on problem-solving transfer tests, and where a study split readers by experience the chapter took the low-experience comparison. The chapter does not say how much of the evidence is its first author's: by our count of the tables, 19 of the 23 coherence tests and 7 of the 28 signalling tests have Mayer as an author. Its limitations section says "more experienced learners may require a greater amount of detail". The redundancy and contiguity sections were not read closely. |
| Tversky (2011), "Visualizing Thought", *Topics in Cognitive Science* 3, 499–535. Second pass, read in place of *Mind in Motion*: the article from the author's faculty page; abstract and sections 2.3, 2.4, 3.2, 4.1 and 4.2 read | "Things that are closer conceptually are placed closer on a page." "Use the vertical for evaluative dimensions, mapping increases upwards. Use the horizontal for neutral dimensions, especially time, mapping increases in reading order. Use dots for entities, lines for relations, arrows for asymmetric relations, boxes for collections." Arrows' "ambiguity can cause misconceptions and confusion". "Step-by-step presentation is more congruent to the way the mind understands and represents continuous organized action than continuous presentation." | A review. The horizontal mapping follows reading order, so it differs between cultures, and the mappings "are incomplete and variable". Its claim about animation is that it has "not proved to be superior to static graphics", not that it is worse. |
| Hegarty, "Capacity Limits in Mechanical Reasoning" (QR 2001 workshop paper), from the Northwestern QRG archive | "Mental animation is conceptualized as occurring in a limited-capacity spatial working memory system." People "do not mentally animate the motion of all components of a mechanical system in parallel". Low-spatial-ability people made more errors on mental animation, not slower. | A simulation plus reviewed studies, not a new experiment; pulley systems. It does **not** study expertise, and says nothing about misconceptions from static versus animated displays. |

| Heiser & Tversky (2005), "Characterizing diagrams produced by individuals and dyads", *Spatial Cognition IV* | Full text. | The introduction states: "Effective diagrams abstract the essential information, and omit information that is irrelevant to the problem or task at hand", and that "for conveying how to operate systems or how systems operate, diagrams are especially effective". **These are statements in the introduction**, citing other work. Learners given control "rarely chose to pause on the frames that were of most relevance". He does not say to prefer stills, and expects well-designed animation to help. ## Author's synthesis

These are judgments built on the sources, not findings of any one of them:

- Choosing the form from the idea's structure as a list of forms (rule 1). Congruence is the
  principle; the table of forms is the skill's.
- Hierarchy by "the smallest effective difference" (rule 3): the principle comes from
  apprehension and signalling; the phrase is Tufte via Casselman, quoted in
  `pedagogical-writing`.
- Prefer a stepper to an animation for a mechanism (rule 6): Hegarty's 2003 report found a
  few frames equal to an animation, not better, and Tversky (2011) argues that steps fit how
  events are understood. The preference itself, and anything about a `<bh-stepper>`, is the
  skill's.
- Several representations (rule 9): "state what each one is for" is the skill's wording, and
  so is making the correspondence visible when the reader must combine two pictures.
  Ainsworth's are using the fewest, showing them together when they must be related, and
  signalling the mapping when one explains the other.
- Later figures of a series can be compact (rule 10). Mayer & Gallini show what novices need,
  not what suits readers who know the topic.
- Mapping the process principles onto `<bh-stepper>`, `<bh-versions>` and `<bh-sequence>`.
- The review checklist.

## Checked and dropped

The issue that started this skill (#80) listed these. The first pass dropped each one because
the source could not be matched. The second pass restored or confirmed some, as marked; the
rest are still not in the skill:

- **Expertise reversal from Kalyuga et al. (1999) and Jian & Wu (2016).** Neither paper has it.
  The rule now uses Mayer & Gallini directly (second pass), which shows no reversal either:
  high-knowledge readers did not need the illustrations and were not hurt by them.
- **Hegarty: misconceptions from static and animated displays alike.** Restored in the second
  pass: the DTIC report says it, and the claim is back in [process-diagrams.md](process-diagrams.md).
- **Ainsworth's three function names** (complement, constrain, construct): restored in the
  second pass, from the author's manuscript.
- **Tufte's layering and separation, micro/macro, small multiples, and "show causality" as
  Tufte's principles.** The books were not available. Small multiples already appears in
  `pedagogical-writing` marked secondary. His own site says the opposite about causality:
  arrows need evidence. **Still unread after the second pass:** *Envisioning Information*
  (1990) and *Visual Explanations* (1997) exist online only as controlled-lending scans,
  which need an account to borrow. They need a library or a bought copy.
- **Tversky, *Mind in Motion* (2019)**: no source reachable in either pass. Her 2011 article
  was read in its place (above); nothing is attributed to the book.
- **Lowe (2003)**: unreachable in the second pass. The publisher's copy is closed, the indexes
  list no open copy, and the web archive holds none. Lowe's 2004 summary of it was read
  instead.
- **Mayer's effect sizes as "d".** Confirmed in the second pass: the chapter computes Cohen's
  d on transfer tests.
- **Mayer, *Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed., 2020)**: not looked for, since the handbook
  chapter gave the effect sizes and their limits.

## Looked at and left out

- **Kosslyn**, *Graph Design for the Eye and Mind*; **Cleveland & McGill** and **Bertin** on
  perceptual encoding: these are about charts, and belong to the `dataviz` skill.
- **Holmes and Cairo** on explanation graphics: peripheral to teaching diagrams, and contested.
- **Amit Patel's "Diagram structure"** is about implementation, not teaching design.
